# Pair precon pricing with layout optimization tools

"Precon Manager" cannot optimize a hospital floor plan for nurse walking, because no product by that exact name could be found and every candidate it refers to prices designs rather than generating or scoring them. The phrase is a buyer-persona label. Beck Technology's "For Preconstruction Managers & VPs" page sits at /precon-manager and promotes its DESTINI estimating platform ([Beck Technology](https://www.beck-technology.com/precon-manager)), and ConCntric markets a page "For Precon and Design Managers" ([ConCntric](https://concntric.com/solutions/precon-managers/)). These tools estimate cost, run takeoffs, level bids and compare design options on cost. Their real job in a nurse-walking project is to price layout alternatives that generative-design, space-syntax and simulation tools have already produced and scored. Published research on the question itself is substantial and goes back more than 50 years. It runs from the 1970 Trites radial-versus-corridor study, through operations-research layout models dating to 1977, to a large RTLS time-and-motion study in 2008, to agent-based simulations published in 2020–2025. That research has a counterintuitive message: overall unit shape is a weak predictor of walking. Where medications and supplies sit relative to patient rooms, and how each nurse's assignment is spread across the unit, matter more. Decentralizing nurse stations without decentralizing supplies tends to increase walking and to erode teamwork. "Shortest possible path" is also the wrong sole objective, because it conflicts with visibility, communication, daylight and infection control. One caveat applies throughout. The research team could not open most primary papers and worked from abstracts, review summaries and search snippets, so the specific figures below are flagged where they need checking against the original sources before anyone relies on them.

## "Precon Manager" is a role label, and precon tools price layouts rather than draw them

No standalone commercial product named "Precon Manager" or "PreconManager" turned up. The strongest match is Beck Technology's persona page, which search snippets say promotes DESTINI Cloud for estimating, takeoff, bidding, analytics and collaboration ([Beck Technology](https://www.beck-technology.com/precon-manager)). The vendor pages could not be fetched directly, so feature details are lower-confidence. DESTINI Estimator is the closest any of these tools comes to design analysis. Capterra lists a **"Scenario and Alternate Analysis" feature for quick cost comparisons of design options**, used by estimators building conceptual budgets ([Capterra](https://www.capterra.com/p/161842/DESTINI-Estimator/)). Its BIM link runs through **Autodesk BIM 360 for 3D quantity takeoff** and through Procore for 2D takeoff and financials ([Beck Technology integrations](https://www.beck-technology.com/destini-estimator-integrations)). Those are quantity and cost links, not spatial analysis. ConCntric describes itself as an AI-native preconstruction platform that builds rough, conceptual and schematic-design estimates from historical data and tracks design-cost alignment ([ConCntric](https://concntric.com/solutions/precon-managers/)). The wider category, including PreconSuite, Precon Playbook, Procore Estimating, Ediphi, Buildr, Zebel and HCSS HeavyBid Pre-Construction, covers bidding, estimating, documents, resourcing and process ([G2 – PreconSuite](https://www.g2.com/products/preconsuite/discuss); [Procore Marketplace](https://marketplace.procore.com/apps/procore-estimating); [ENR – Ediphi](https://www.enr.com/articles/59200-ediphi-creates-platform-for-cloud-based-precon-estimates)). Autodesk and Procore both define preconstruction software as estimating, bid management and scheduling during planning ([Autodesk](https://www.autodesk.com/solutions/preconstruction-software); [Procore](https://www.procore.com/library/preconstruction-software)).

None of these platforms advertises floor-plan generation, travel-distance analysis, or operational simulation. The research found no integration between any precon vendor and simulation tools such as AnyLogic, FlexSim or MedModel, or generative tools such as Revit Generative Design, Hypar or TestFit. A walking-distance metric would therefore have to be entered by hand into the precon tool, for example as an alternate note or custom property. A DESTINI user's review sets a further limit: early in design "I can't just rely on BIM models. My estimate depends on the quality of the BIM model" ([G2 – DESTINI reviews](https://www.g2.com/products/destini-estimator/reviews)). The cost side of a layout comparison is only as reliable as the models the design team hands over. If "Precon Manager" refers to an in-house or small regional tool, it could not be identified. In that case, check its feature list against this finding.

## Fifty years of studies say nurses walk miles, but shape explains little of it

The answer to "are there published studies?" is yes, many. The largest is **Hendrich et al. (2008), a time-and-motion study of 767 nurses on 36 medical-surgical units in 15 US states** that combined PDA self-report with real-time location tracking ([Permanente Journal](https://www.thepermanentejournal.org/doi/10.7812/tpp/08-021)). Documentation took **35.3% of practice time** and patient care activities only **19.3%** ([Semantic Scholar summary](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/A-36-hospital-time-and-motion-study:-how-do-nurses-Hendrich-Chow/d1a9b35a5a28862c3ce6db07cf7b4a79df279b11)). Trade press attributes to the same study **2.4–3.4 miles per 10-hour day shift and 1.3–3.3 miles per night shift**, with distance varying **fourfold or more even within a single unit** ([Healthcare Design](https://healthcaredesignmagazine.com/?p=1944)). Those distance figures need verification, because the PMC abstract of the paper states that distance findings were excluded from that article owing to complications in data analysis ([PMC3037121](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3037121)). The widely repeated "five-plus miles per shift" claims come largely from uncontrolled pedometer counts. One local project reported nurses walking "at least six miles every eight hours" ([The Daily Record](https://thedailyrecord.com/2008/07/07/anne-arundel-county-nurses-track-their-mileage/)). Pedometers also count steps unrelated to work, so they likely overstate work-related walking compared with location tracking. An earlier article titled "How far do nurses walk?" is indexed as PubMed 16999182 ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16999182/)). The research notes disagree on whether it appeared in *Medsurg Nursing* or *Nursing Economics* and who wrote it, so confirm the citation before using it.

The fourfold within-unit spread is the most important number in this literature for anyone hoping to design walking away. It means workload, call lights and assignment geography drive much of the variance, not just the building. A 2025 Japanese study of 14 wards confirms this. **Nurse calls increased walking on every shift**, patient-to-nurse ratio mattered only at night, and "ward characteristics substantially affected walking distance" ([Wiley – Nakashima et al. 2025](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/jonm/5540600)). The spatial reanalysis of the Hendrich dataset found **no difference between unit types in time spent in patient rooms**. However, nurses whose assignments were **more central to all their rooms entered patients' rooms more often and spent more total time there** ([SAGE – Hendrich et al. 2009](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/193758670900200202)).

The shape debate started with **Trites et al. (1970)**, which observed 590 staff over 82 days across radial, single-corridor and double-corridor units at Baptist Memorial Hospital in Memphis. Radial units produced the **least travel and the most patient time**, and a review summary gives day-shift walking of roughly **0.61 miles for radial units, 0.79 for double-corridor units and 1.08 for single-corridor units** ([CHD – Trites et al.](https://www.healthdesign.org/sites/default/files/article-pdfs/Trites_Galbraith_Sturdavant_Leckwart-1970-CHD-KPS.pdf)). Those mileage figures come from a secondary review and need checking against the 1970 paper. The study was also confounded, because the units served different service lines and had different room mixes ([Pachilova & Sailer 2020](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10097438/1/Pachilova_Sailer_2020_ProvidingCareQualitybyDesign_JOA_published.pdf)). Later work undercut the "radial is best" conclusion. **Yi and Seo (2012)** shadowed medication rounds on a hospital with a radial wing and found the **radial wing produced longer paths and more deviations**. They concluded that overall unit shape "might not be a good predictor of nurses' walking behavior" and that the path between patient room and medication area predicts better ([PubMed 23224843](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23224843/)). That study was small (12 nurses, 20 observations, corridor walking only). Compactness can even add steps. Smaller, more visible units generate **extra stops and spontaneous interactions** that lengthen walks ([CHD EBD review](https://www.healthdesign.org/sites/default/files/LitReviewWP_FINAL.pdf)). Shorter distances do not automatically mean less time walking.

|---|---|---|---|

## Decentralized stations backfire unless supplies and medications move with them

Decentralized nurse stations were meant to bring nurses closer to patients and shorten their walks. Most pre/post studies found the opposite. In a quasi-experimental study of three units, **walking distance rose significantly on two of three units after decentralization**, and self-reported collaboration fell even as nurses rated the building's support for collaboration higher ([PubMed 25816381 – Pati et al. 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25816381/)). One research note attributes this paper to Fay, Cai and Real, but the PubMed record supports Pati et al. A cardiovascular-unit evaluation found decentralized stations **increased overall walking**, increased time in patient rooms and cut time at stations. Nurses rated the centralized design higher for teamwork and efficient care, and only the centralized station gave **100% nurse-to-nurse visibility** ([CHD – Fay et al. 2017](https://www.healthdesign.org/knowledge-repository/shifting-landscapes-impact-centralized-and-decentralized-nursing-station-models)). Zborowsky et al. (2010) found consultations with medical staff and social interactions **significantly less frequent at decentralized stations**, and **neither model gave better patient visibility** ([CHD summary](https://healthdesign.org/knowledge-repository/centralized-vs-decentralized-nursing-stations-effects-nurses%E2%80%99-functional-use)). A 2019 systematic review found teamwork perceived to decline under decentralization while patient experience trended positively ([SAGE – Fay, Cai & Real 2019](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1937586718805222)). Staff at a new decentralized trauma hospital said the change hurt teamwork, social support and mentoring ([CHD – Real et al. 2017](https://www.healthdesign.org/sites/default/files/article-pdfs/Real-2017-CHD-KPS.pdf)).

The explanation runs through supplies. Moving the charting desk does nothing if nurses still walk to a central medication room, clean utility room and colleagues. Reviews summarizing Hendrich's work report that decentralized stations reduced walking **especially when supplies were also decentralized**, and that **centralized supplies "could double staff walking"** whatever the station type ([CHD 21st Century Hospital review](https://www.healthdesign.org/sites/default/files/Role%20Physical%20Environ%20in%20the%2021st%20Century%20Hospital_0.pdf)). The simulation figures are the largest in the literature. A CADRE simulation that computes walking for any floor layout found a decentralized model cut **estimated total walking per RN by 26.8%** ([CADRE](https://www.cadreresearch.org/projects/towards-optimizing-inpatient-unit-design-and-operation)). The same review literature reports a room-side "nurse server" model cutting walking by **67.9% per RN per shift** relative to centralized supplies ([CHD review](https://www.healthdesign.org/sites/default/files/Role%20Physical%20Environ%20in%20the%2021st%20Century%20Hospital_0.pdf)). The 67.9% figure has not been traced to its primary authors and needs verification. A MedModel study by D. Pati compared nurse servers with a central medication room and found **576 feet less medication-related walking per 12-hour shift**, a result that led Houston Medical Center to adopt the design for a new bed tower ([CADRE – MedModel study](https://cadreresearch.org/projects/a-medmodel-decision-support-study)). The practical rule follows directly. The biggest walking lever is putting medications and supplies where the care happens, through decentralized alcoves and in-room servers, and pairing any station decentralization with team hubs to protect communication. Evidence that same-handed rooms standardize workflow is "currently scarce" ([HFM](https://www.hfmmagazine.com/articles/1049-patient-focus)).

## Optimization math has solved hospital layouts since 1977, but rarely at the nursing-unit scale

Operations research treats hospital layout as a **Quadratic Assignment Problem (QAP)**: assign departments to locations so that the sum of trip frequency multiplied by distance is as small as possible. **Elshafei (1977)** framed hospital department placement this way ([JORS](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1057%2Fjors.1977.29.pdf)). His 19-facility instance became the QAPLIB benchmark els19 ([QAP survey](https://coral.ise.lehigh.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/07/QAP_Survey_04.pdf)). The 30-department layout problem from Klinikum Regensburg, known as Krarup30a, remained open for decades until **Hahn and Krarup (2001) proved it optimal with branch-and-bound** ([IAOR](https://iaorifors.com/paper/51772)). That history shows the main limit. The QAP is NP-hard, and exact solutions top out around 30 units. Real hospitals therefore use decomposition or heuristics. **Helber et al. (2016)** used a hierarchical QAP with a fix-and-optimize heuristic on Hannover Medical School data to place departments and wards across existing buildings ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10696-015-9214-6)). Arnolds and Nickel built multi-period ward layout models ([EconBiz](https://econbiz.de/Record/multi-period-layout-planning-for-hospital-wards-arnolds-ines-verena/10009792022)). They also built a loop in which an optimizer proposes room assignments and **discrete-event simulation tests them against uncertain patient flows**, including fairness across patient types ([WSC 2012](https://www.informs-sim.org/wsc12papers/includes/files/pos183.pdf)). Two refinements make the objective more realistic. One derives trip frequencies from **mined clinical pathways** rather than guesses ([Arnolds & Gartner 2018](https://unpaywall.org/10.1007%2FS10479-018-2799-X)). The other measures **geodesic distances** along the actual walkable corridor network ([TU Delft](https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:2d1d3c0f-18ae-42a0-a379-e698f5586400)).

At the nursing-unit scale the decision variable usually shifts from walls to people. A 2024 *OR Spectrum* model jointly optimizes patient-to-room and nurse-to-patient assignment because together they determine nurse walking ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00291-024-00800-z)). Constraint-programming work treats a nurse's round as a shortest Hamiltonian cycle, which is a traveling-salesman problem, through assigned rooms ([Univ. Ferrara](https://iris.unife.it/retrieve/e309ade0-39a4-3969-e053-3a05fe0a2c94/ICLP12-DagstuhlProceedings.pdf)). That is the literal "shortest path for rounds" problem, but it is an operational problem solved shift by shift, not a design problem. Design determines the distance matrix. Assignment and routing decide how much of it each nurse walks.

Simulation is how researchers compare unit layouts. An agent-based model of **8 Korean medical-surgical layouts with 33 walking routes** found that a central station with multi-bed rooms placed nearer to it **cut simulated walking by 15%**, though only for a single scenario without acuity or nurse experience ([CHD – Lee, Lee & Shepley 2020](https://healthdesign.org/sites/default/files/article-pdfs/Lee-2020-CHD-KPS.pdf)). HKS ran simulation after each design iteration for ProMedica and reported that the final plan **halved walking distances despite a larger floor plate**, though the result had not been validated after occupancy ([Healthcare Design](https://healthcaredesignmagazine.com/trends/put-test/14436/)). Generative-design examples remain proofs of concept. Examples include PSO-generated layouts converted to BIM ([MDPI Buildings 2024](https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/buildings/buildings-14-02354/article_deploy/buildings-14-02354-v2.pdf?version=1722416004)), MIP room and corridor generation ([TU Delft](https://repository.tudelft.nl/file/File_0dd30a3c-7fd2-4f54-b60a-980ae1a21847?preview=1)), and graph-based near-optimal layouts that matched expert scores ([Penn State](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/implementation-and-evaluation-of-generative-layout-options-using-/)). The research found **no published post-occupancy study confirming that an optimized layout delivered its predicted walking reduction**. That is the field's largest gap.

## Shortest walking loses to a multi-objective brief built on five tools

Minimizing walking alone produces bad hospitals. Visibility is the clearest conflict. Lu and Zimring's **Targeted Visibility Index**, which counts only views of patient beds, scored radial units highest ([CHD – Lu 2010](https://healthdesign.org/system/files/Lu-2010-CHD-KPS.pdf)). Yet radial geometry did not shorten real walks in Yi and Seo's study. A Korean comparison found an I-shaped station gave a panoramic view but "very rigorous walking" ([koreascience](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO201515337344118.pdf)). A review cautions that patient safety through visibility is "more complex than clear lines of sight from the nursing station to the patient" ([PMC4287700](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4287700)). Decentralization trades walking and teamwork for patient proximity, as shown above. Code and clinical requirements add further constraints, including exterior windows in patient rooms, single rooms, hand-washing locations, clean/soiled separation and acoustic control. The US FGI Guidelines set many of these, but the specific clauses were not retrieved and must be checked against the current edition. No accessible source quantified the walking-versus-visibility trade-off numerically, so any project will have to compute its own Pareto front.

The workflow below follows from that evidence and from the tools that exist today. The precon platform enters at the end.

Static shortest-path and space-syntax metrics are cheap enough to run inside a generative loop over thousands of options. Discrete-event and agent-based simulation capture variability from workload and call lights, which accounts for the fourfold spread in real walking. They are expensive to set up, so they belong at the shortlist stage. The Autodesk University hospital pharmacy project at Saint-Louis Hospital is a warning about scope. The team optimized material and people flows across roughly 1,300 m² and reported that the over-constrained site made it a poor first generative-design project ([Autodesk University](https://www.autodesk.com/autodesk-university/article/Generative-Design-Complex-Buildings-Optimizing-Spaces-and-Flows-Dynamo-and-Refinery-2020)). TestFit, Finch and Autodesk Forma had no documented healthcare or nurse-travel use, and Simio and Arena were not researched.

## Conclusion

The question is best reframed from "shortest path" to "fewest unnecessary trips, without losing sight of patients or colleagues." The evidence suggests the wins come from locating medications and supplies next to where care happens, keeping each nurse's assignment geographically clustered, and protecting team hubs. Choosing a radial floor plate does far less. Simulated gains run from 15% to roughly 68%, and the 50% HKS figure sits in the same range. All of them are model predictions or unverified reports, and no one has published a post-occupancy study confirming that an optimized layout delivered its promised savings. A project that measures nurse walking before and after occupancy with RTLS would therefore add genuinely new evidence, not just confirm what is known.

For the tool question, the preconstruction platform is the last step in the chain. Its value is turning each layout's operational benefit into a cost-versus-benefit decision. Hours of nurse walking saved per shift, multiplied over a building's life, can be set against the capital cost of extra medication alcoves or nurse servers. No precon tool computes that benefit today. The team should treat it as an input supplied by the design and simulation stack. Before any figure in this report goes into a business case, check it against the original paper, particularly the Hendrich walking distances, the Trites mileages and the 67.9% nurse-server reduction.
