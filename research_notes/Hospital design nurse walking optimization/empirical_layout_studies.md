## 1. How far do nurses walk per shift, and how much time goes to walking vs. direct care?

### Takeaway
The largest study, Hendrich et al. 2008 (767 nurses, 36 med-surg units), found nurses walk roughly 1–5 miles per 10-hour shift. Distance varied about fourfold, even within a single unit. Only about 19% of nurse time went to direct patient care activities, while documentation took about 35%. Pedometer studies of local nurses report much higher numbers, but they are weaker evidence.

### Cited Findings
- **Hendrich A, Chow MP, Skierczynski BA, Lu Z. (2008). "A 36-Hospital Time and Motion Study: How Do Medical-Surgical Nurses Spend Their Time?" *The Permanente Journal* 12(3):25–34. doi:10.7812/tpp/08-021.** The study covered 767 nurses on 36 medical-surgical units in 17 health systems and 15 states. It used PDA self-report of activities combined with RTLS location tracking. — [Permanente Journal](https://www.thepermanentejournal.org/doi/10.7812/tpp/08-021); [Purdue repository](https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1060&context=rche_rp)
  - Documentation was the largest share of practice time at **35.3% (~147.5 min/shift)**. Care coordination took **20.6%** and medication administration **17.2%**. — [Semantic Scholar record/summary](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/A-36-hospital-time-and-motion-study:-how-do-nurses-Hendrich-Chow/d1a9b35a5a28862c3ce6db07cf7b4a79df279b11)
  - Patient care activities took only **19.3% of nurse time (~81 min/shift)**. One summary reports that travel to fetch supplies took about **36 min/shift**. This is secondary and not verified against the original. — [CHD EBD literature review summary](https://www.healthdesign.org/sites/default/files/LitReviewWP_FINAL.pdf)
  - Walking on the day shift was **2.4–3.4 miles per 10 h** (one excerpt gives a median of about 3.0 mi). The night shift was **1.3–3.3 miles per 10 h**. Distance per shift varied by a factor of **4 or more, even within the same unit**. Extrapolated to a 12-h shift, the upper end is about 4 miles. — [Healthcare Design, "Designing smart"](https://healthcaredesignmagazine.com/?p=1944); [Permanente Journal PDF](https://www.thepermanentejournal.org/doi/pdf/10.7812/tpp/08-021). The search snippet values were hard to parse, so verify them against the full text. A secondary blog converts them to medians of about 4.8 km (day) and 3.5 km (night) per 10 h — [Optium blog (secondary)](https://www.optium.com.tr/en/blogs/how-far-do-nurses-walk-12-hour-shift)
  - The study's main targets for efficiency were **documentation, medication administration and care coordination**. — [Purdue repository](https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/rche_rp/50)
- **Hendrich A, Chow MP, Bafna S, Choudhary R, Heo Y, Skierczynski BA. (2009). "Unit-Related Factors That Affect Nursing Time With Patients: Spatial Analysis of the Time and Motion Study." *HERD* 2(2):5–20. doi:10.1177/193758670900200202.** This is a spatial reanalysis of the same 767-nurse dataset.
  - Initial analysis **did not detect differences between unit types in time spent in patient rooms**.
  - Nurse assignments that were more **central to all their assigned rooms** had more entries into patient rooms and to the nurse station. More room entries meant shorter visits but **more total time in patient rooms**.
  - The authors conclude that the spatial qualities of assignments and layout shape how nurses move through units. — [SAGE abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/193758670900200202)
- **"How far do nurses walk?" *Medsurg Nursing* 2006;15(4):213–6 (PMID 16999182).** This is a prospective time-and-motion/walking study. The PubMed record has no abstract, and the search could not confirm the author list (often attributed to Welton et al.). — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16999182/)
  - A 2008 news report cites this article alongside a local pedometer project where "most nurses walked at least six miles every eight hours." That was a separate, non-peer-reviewed local measurement. — [The Daily Record, 2008](https://thedailyrecord.com/2008/07/07/anne-arundel-county-nurses-track-their-mileage/)
- **Nakashima K, et al. (2025). "Factors Affecting Nurses' Walking Distance: Age, Clinical Ladder Level, Wards, Nurse Calls, Weekend, and Patient-To-Nurse Ratio." *Journal of Nursing Management* (Wiley/Hindawi), doi:10.1155/jonm/5540600.** The study covered 14 wards in Japan, with nurses aged 22–60, analyzed by nonparametric tests and multiple regression.
  - The **number of nurse calls** significantly increased walking distance on **all** shifts.
  - **Patient-to-nurse ratio** mattered only on **night** shifts, and **weekends** only on long-day shifts.
  - **Ward characteristics "substantially affected walking distance."**
  - Clinical ladder level showed small-to-medium effect differences. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/jonm/5540600); [PMC12411061](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12411061/)
- **Yi L, Seo HB. (2012).** This study observed task-level walking during medication administration (see Section 2). Experienced nurses made more unnecessary stops and walked longer than new nurses because of social and work interactions. — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-Effect-of-Hospital-Unit-Layout-on-Nurse-Walking-Yi-Seo/035376abeadbee5e49134bea651642c082b918a0)
- A 2025 HERD paper, "The Impact of Hospital Design on Time Spent on Nursing Tasks" (doi:10.1177/19375867251330838), exists, but its findings were not retrievable. — [Ovid](https://www.ovid.com/journals/herdj/fulltext/10.1177/19375867251330838~the-impact-of-hospital-design-on-time-spent-on-nursing-tasks)

### Inferences
- Per-shift distance in rigorous RTLS-based US med-surg data is mostly **1–5 miles per 10 h**. Claims of "5+ miles per shift" in trade media come mostly from uncontrolled pedometer counts, which also pick up non-work steps.
- The fourfold variation within a single unit means **workload, assignment geography and nurse calls** explain as much variance as building shape. This argues for studying assignment patterns alongside layout.
- Walking is a modest share of time compared with documentation. Its design relevance is that walking fragments care and reduces patient-room time.

### Gaps
- I could not verify the exact Hendrich 2008 walking median and percentiles, or the share of time classified as "walking," against the full text.
- I could not obtain the authors and numerical results of the 2006 *Medsurg Nursing* article.
- I found no reliable meta-analytic pooled estimate of per-shift walking distance.

## 2. How do unit shapes compare (single-corridor, double-corridor/racetrack, radial, triangular, cluster)?

### Takeaway
The seminal Trites et al. 1970 study found radial units produced the least walking and the most patient time, with double-corridor second and single-corridor worst. Later, better-controlled work (Yi & Seo 2012; Hendrich et al. 2009) found that overall shape is a weak predictor of walking. What matters more are path characteristics, such as the distance from rooms to medications, supplies and the station, and the centrality of each nurse's assignment.

### Cited Findings
- **Trites DK, Galbraith FD, Sturdavant M, Leckwart JF. (1970). "Influence of nursing-unit design on the activities and subjective feelings of nursing personnel." *Environment and Behavior* 2(3):303–334.** Sometimes dated 1969. The study was set at Baptist Memorial Hospital, Memphis. — [CHD PDF](https://www.healthdesign.org/sites/default/files/article-pdfs/Trites_Galbraith_Sturdavant_Leckwart-1970-CHD-KPS.pdf)
  - It compared **4 radial, 4 single-corridor and 4 double-corridor units** and observed **590 staff over 82 days** with work-sampling.
  - Radial units had the **least travel and the most time with patients**. Reported day-shift walking was **0.61 miles (radial)** vs **0.79 miles (double-corridor)** vs **1.08 miles (single-corridor)**. These figures come from a review summary.
  - Ranking: radial best, double-corridor next. Most staff preferred radial units and believed they improved care quality. A review adds that **72% of transferred patients preferred radial over double-corridor**.
  - **Confounds:** the units served different service lines, and single-corridor units had more rooms and a higher share of single rooms. — summarized in [Trites et al. via CHD](https://www.healthdesign.org/sites/default/files/article-pdfs/Trites_Galbraith_Sturdavant_Leckwart-1970-CHD-KPS.pdf) and [Pachilova & Sailer 2020, *J. of Architecture*](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10097438/1/Pachilova_Sailer_2020_ProvidingCareQualitybyDesign_JOA_published.pdf)
- **Shepley MM, Davies K. (2003). "Nursing Unit Configuration and Its Relationship to Noise and Nurse Walking Behavior: An AIDS/HIV Unit Case Study." *AIA Academy Journal of Architecture for Health* 6.** It compared a radial with a linear/rectangular unit. The hypotheses were that staff walk more on the large rectangular units and that the compact radial design has less noise. — [Brikbase PDF](https://www.brikbase.org/sites/default/files/aah_journal_v6_2003_oct_03_0.pdf). I could not retrieve the results (see Gaps).
- **Yi L, Seo HB. (2012). "The Effect of Hospital Unit Layout on Nurse Walking Behavior." *HERD* 6(1):66–82. doi:10.1177/193758671200600104.** This was an observational shadowing study of medication administration on a unit with a radial east wing and a west wing (**12 nurses, 20 observations**).
  - The **radial wing produced longer walking paths and more deviations** than the other wing, and the smaller wing had more walking than the larger one. This is the opposite of shape-based expectations.
  - Conclusion: "overall unit shape or unit layout type might not be a good predictor of nurses' walking behavior". The **path connecting functional spaces (patient room ↔ medication area)** predicts better.
  - Only corridor walking was observed, not walking inside rooms. — [PubMed 23224843](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23224843/); [CHD KPS](https://www.healthdesign.org/system/files/Yi_Seo-2012-CHD-KPS.pdf); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-Effect-of-Hospital-Unit-Layout-on-Nurse-Walking-Yi-Seo/035376abeadbee5e49134bea651642c082b918a0)
- **Hendrich et al. (2009), HERD 2(2).** The study found no difference by unit type in patient-room time. **Assignment centrality** drove room entries and total time in rooms. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/193758670900200202)
- **Seo HB, Choi YS, Zimring C. (2011). "Impact of hospital unit design for patient-centered care on nurses' behavior." *Environment and Behavior*.** A CHD white paper cites it, with Lu & Seo, for the finding that smaller units give nurses more visibility of each other. Higher visibility then leads to **more walking via extra stops and spontaneous interactions**. — [CHD EBD white paper](https://www.healthdesign.org/sites/default/files/LitReviewWP_FINAL.pdf); [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/258132379_Impact_of_Hospital_Unit_Design_for_Patient-Centered_Care_on_Nurses'_Behavior)
- **Lee (2020), agent-based simulation.** It modeled **8 Korean med-surg layouts (4 rectangular, 4 triangular)** for nurse walking. Its numerical results were not retrieved. — [CHD KPS Lee 2020](https://healthdesign.org/sites/default/files/article-pdfs/Lee-2020-CHD-KPS.pdf)
- **Pachilova R, Sailer K. (2020). "Providing care quality by design." *The Journal of Architecture*.** This is a space-syntax study that reviews the Trites lineage and analyzes ward layouts. — [UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10097438/1/Pachilova_Sailer_2020_ProvidingCareQualitybyDesign_JOA_published.pdf)
- Recent related work, with abstract-level information only:
  - A 2023 *Buildings* (MDPI) study on ward layout and nurse-round efficiency in China's cold regions — [MDPI](https://mdpi.com/2075-5309/13/6/1399)
  - A HERD paper "Optimizing Nurse Workflow Efficiency: An Examination of Nurse Walking" (BSA LifeStructures summary, 2024). It recommends open layouts with transparent partitions for team visibility — [BSA summary PDF](https://www.bsadesign.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/Summary-Page_HERD-Journal_Optimizing-Nurse-Workflow-2.pdf)
  - Sailer/Pachilova-type research, "Design lessons from the analysis of nurse journeys in a hospital ward" — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/321973829_Design_lessons_from_the_analysis_of_nurse_journeys_in_a_hospital_ward)

### Inferences
- The "radial is best" result from Trites is confounded by service line and room mix, and has not been cleanly replicated. Newer studies point to **functional adjacency and walk-path design** within any shape.
- Compact or visible layouts can paradoxically add walking through social stops. Reducing distance is not the same as reducing walking time.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve Shepley & Davies 2003 results, Lee 2020 numbers, or the HERD "Optimizing Nurse Workflow" quantitative results.
- I found no reliable primary-source numbers for triangular or cluster/pod units in the US (e.g., the Cai & Zimring work on unit layout and communication). The Cai & Zimring findings were not retrieved.
- The Trites miles figures come from a secondary review and need checking against the 1970 paper.

## 3. Centralized vs. decentralized nurse stations: walking, visibility, teamwork, isolation

### Takeaway
Decentralized stations (DNS) were expected to cut walking. Most empirical pre/post studies found the opposite: walking stayed the same or increased, nurses spent more time in or near patient rooms, and teamwork and consultation declined. Visibility results are mixed. Walking falls only when supplies and medications are decentralized along with the charting stations.

### Cited Findings
- **Zborowsky T, Bunker-Hellmich L, Morelli A, O'Neill M. (2010). "Centralized vs. decentralized nursing stations: Effects on nurses' functional use of space and work environment." *HERD* 3(4):19–42.** This was an exploratory mixed-methods study.
  - **Consultations with medical staff and social interactions were significantly less frequent at decentralized stations.**
  - **Neither design gave superior patient visibility.**
  - Sound levels exceeded recommendations at all stations on all shifts.
  - There were no significant differences in perceived work control, demand or support.
  - The authors recommend a **hybrid model**: decentralized stations plus centralized meeting or consult rooms.
  - Limitations: small sample, no longitudinal data, and confounding by unit type and hospital size. — [CHD summary](https://healthdesign.org/knowledge-repository/centralized-vs-decentralized-nursing-stations-effects-nurses%E2%80%99-functional-use); [Healthcare Design](https://healthcaredesignmagazine.com/?p=776)
- **Pati D, Harvey TE Jr, Redden P, Summers B, Pati S. (2015). "An empirical examination of the impacts of decentralized nursing unit design." *HERD* 8(2):56–70.** This was a multimethod, before-after quasi-experimental study of **3 units** (data from spring and fall 2011). It measured time use, walking, acute stress, productivity and teamwork.
  - **Walking distance increased significantly on 2 of 3 units** after decentralization.
  - Self-reported **collaboration decreased**, even though ratings of the facility's support for collaboration increased.
  - The authors note that visibility, substations and medication location can reduce walking. — [PubMed 25816381](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25816381/)
- **Fay L, Carll-White A, Schadler A, Isaacs KB, Real K. (2017). "Shifting Landscapes: The Impact of Centralized and Decentralized Nursing Station Models on the Efficiency of Care." *HERD* 10(5):80–94. doi:10.1177/1937586717698812.** This was a pre/post occupancy evaluation of a cardiovascular service line at an academic hospital.
  - Methods: pedometers, room-use data, time studies, visibility counts and questionnaires.
  - The DNS model **increased overall walking distances**, increased visits to and time in patient rooms, and **decreased time at nurse stations**.
  - The centralized design was rated significantly higher for supporting **teamwork and efficient care with decreased walking**.
  - The centralized station allowed **100% nurse-to-nurse visibility**, while more visible beds were possible only with DNS.
  - Supply stations placed near the decentralized stations are suggested to offset the extra walking. — [CHD knowledge repository](https://www.healthdesign.org/knowledge-repository/shifting-landscapes-impact-centralized-and-decentralized-nursing-station-models); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/315733444_Shifting_Landscapes_The_Impact_of_Centralized_and_Decentralized_Nursing_Station_Models_on_the_Efficiency_of_Care); [UKY Scholars](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/shifting-landscapes-the-impact-of-centralized-and-decentralized-n/)
- **Real K, Bardach SH, Bardach DR. (2017). "The role of the built environment: How decentralized nurse stations shape communication, patient care processes, and patient outcomes." *Health Communication*.** This was a mixed-methods comparison of a new DNS trauma hospital with the old centralized unit.
  - Nurses, techs and managers said DNS **adversely affected teamwork, social support and mentoring**.
  - Walking inefficiencies were **"improved in some ways but exacerbated in others."** — [CHD KPS Real 2017](https://www.healthdesign.org/sites/default/files/article-pdfs/Real-2017-CHD-KPS.pdf)
- **Fay L, Cai H, Real K. (2019). "A Systematic Literature Review of Empirical Studies on Decentralized Nursing Stations." *HERD* 12(1).** It found that nursing **teamwork was perceived to decline** with DNS, while **patient experience trended positively**. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1937586718805222); [UKY](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/a-systematic-literature-review-of-empirical-studies-on-decentrali/)
- **Jimenez FE, Puumala SE, Apple M, Bunker-Hellmich LA, Rich RK, Brittin J. (2019). "Associations of Patient and Staff Outcomes With Inpatient Unit Designs Incorporating Decentralized Caregiver Workstations: A Systematic Review of Empirical Evidence." *HERD*.** — [doi:10.1177/1937586718796590](https://doi.org/10.1177/1937586718796590). The specific pooled conclusions were not retrieved.
- **Durham J, Kenyon A. (2019). "Decentralized Nurse Stations: A Methodology for Using Research to Guide Design Decisions." *HERD*.** — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1937586719842356)
- A follow-on study (Fay/Real group, 2020), "Designing for Efficiency: Examining the Impact of Centralized and Decentralized Nurse Stations on Interdisciplinary Care Processes." — [PubMed 32433113](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32433113/). Results were not retrieved.
- **Gharaveis A.** Related work on centralized vs. hybrid stations and teamwork is indexed by CHD ("Centralized/hybrid nurse station, communication and teamwork among nursing staff"). — [CHD](https://healthdesign.org/knowledge-repository/centralized-hybrid-nurse-station-communication-and-teamwork-among-nursing-staff). I could not confirm its authors or numbers from search.

### Inferences
- The evidence is consistent: **DNS alone does not reduce walking**. Nurses still travel to central meds, supplies and colleagues, while DNS shifts presence toward patients.
- The isolation and teamwork costs are the most replicated DNS downside. Hybrid models with team hubs are the common recommendation.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the specific walking magnitudes (feet or steps) from Pati 2015 or Fay 2017.
- I could not verify Gharaveis's inpatient-unit findings. His best-known visibility and teamwork work appears to be in emergency departments, which I have not confirmed.

## 4. Supply and medication room placement, same-handed vs. mirrored rooms, room-to-station distance, unit size

### Takeaway
Decentralizing supplies and medications alongside stations is the most consistently supported lever for reducing walking. Centralized supplies can roughly double walking. Simulation suggests large reductions, from about 27% to about 68%. Evidence that same-handed rooms improve efficiency is scarce. Room-to-medication path length and assignment centrality predict walking better than unit size per se.

### Cited Findings
- Per a CHD EBD review summarizing Hendrich's work, **decentralized stations reduced walking and increased patient-care time, especially when supplies were also decentralized** near the station. Conversely, **centralized supplies "could double staff walking"** and substantially cut care time, regardless of station type. — [CHD "Role of the Physical Environment in the 21st Century Hospital" / Ulrich et al. 2008 review](https://www.healthdesign.org/sites/default/files/Role%20Physical%20Environ%20in%20the%2021st%20Century%20Hospital_0.pdf); [CHD EBD white paper](https://www.healthdesign.org/sites/default/files/LitReviewWP_FINAL.pdf)
- A simulation study cited in the same literature found that, compared with a centralized supply model, **decentralized supplies reduced walking by 26.8%** and a **room/bedside (nurse-server) model by 67.9% per RN per shift**. This came via a search summary, and the primary authors were not confirmed. — [CHD 21st Century Hospital review](https://www.healthdesign.org/sites/default/files/Role%20Physical%20Environ%20in%20the%2021st%20Century%20Hospital_0.pdf)
- **Hendrich A, Fay J, Sorrells A. (2002, 2004). Methodist Hospital, Indianapolis (Clarian Health) Cardiac Comprehensive Critical Care.** This was the acuity-adaptable room project with decentralized stations. Transfers fell **90%** and medication errors fell **67%**. It is often cited for reduced walking and handoffs, but specific walking numbers were not retrieved. — [CHD EBD white paper (Ulrich, Zimring, Zhu, DuBose, Seo 2008)](https://www.healthdesign.org/sites/default/files/LitReviewWP_FINAL.pdf)
- Yi & Seo 2012 found the **path between patient room and medication area** was the key predictor of walking. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23224843/)
- Pati et al. 2015 found that substations and medication location can mitigate DNS walking increases. Medication-room and supply-room use changed consistently across all 3 units after redesign. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25816381/)
- Hendrich et al. 2009 found that more **central assignments** produced more room entries and more total time in rooms. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/193758670900200202)
- Nakashima et al. 2025 found that **patient-to-nurse ratio** increased walking on night shifts only, and nurse calls increased walking on all shifts. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/jonm/5540600)
- **Same-handed vs. mirrored rooms:** The study "Same-Handed and Mirrored Unit Configurations: Is There a Difference in Patient and Nurse Outcomes?" (*JONA*, ~2011) found that same-handed configurations had **lower reported noise, better sleep, and more frequent approach to the patient's right side**. Other sources say several studies show same-handed rooms **do not standardize workflow more than mirrored rooms**, and that supporting evidence is "currently scarce." — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/51163267_Same-Handed_and_Mirrored_Unit_Configurations_Is_There_a_Difference_in_Patient_and_Nurse_Outcomes); [HFM "Patient focus"](https://www.hfmmagazine.com/articles/1049-patient-focus)
- **Smaller units** may raise visibility and spontaneous interactions, which leads to extra stops and walking (Seo, Choi & Zimring 2011; Lu & Seo). — [CHD white paper](https://www.healthdesign.org/sites/default/files/LitReviewWP_FINAL.pdf)

### Inferences
- The strongest design lever is **co-locating supplies and medications with caregivers**, through decentralized supply alcoves and in-room nurse servers, rather than station configuration alone.
- Assignment practices (geographic clustering of a nurse's rooms) are a non-capital lever comparable to layout.

### Gaps
- I found no primary quantitative data retrieved on walking in same-handed vs. mirrored rooms.
- I could not confirm the primary source of the 26.8% / 67.9% simulation figures.
- I found no clean dose-response studies relating unit bed count to walking distance.

## 5. Measurement methods and quality of evidence

### Takeaway
The methods used are pedometers, which are cheap but noisy and include non-work steps; shadowing and work sampling (Trites; Yi & Seo); PDA self-report combined with RTLS (Hendrich 2008); and agent-based or simulation modeling (Lee 2020). Most studies are single-site pre/post or cross-sectional case studies with small samples and confounding by service line, staffing and technology. The evidence quality is moderate-to-low.

### Cited Findings
- Trites 1970 used work-sampling observation of 590 staff, but was confounded by service line and room mix. — [CHD PDF](https://www.healthdesign.org/sites/default/files/article-pdfs/Trites_Galbraith_Sturdavant_Leckwart-1970-CHD-KPS.pdf)
- Hendrich 2008 combined PDAs and RTLS across 767 nurses and 36 units, the largest multisite dataset. — [Permanente Journal](https://www.thepermanentejournal.org/doi/10.7812/tpp/08-021)
- Fay 2017 used pedometers, time studies and visibility counts. — [CHD](https://www.healthdesign.org/knowledge-repository/shifting-landscapes-impact-centralized-and-decentralized-nursing-station-models)
- In a pre-post relocation study, RNs recorded pedometer data at the end of each shift. — search summary of [Pati et al. 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25816381/)
- Yi & Seo 2012 used direct shadowing of 12 nurses and 20 observations, corridor only. Its generalizability is limited. — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-Effect-of-Hospital-Unit-Layout-on-Nurse-Walking-Yi-Seo/035376abeadbee5e49134bea651642c082b918a0)
- Zborowsky 2010 lists as limitations its small sample, lack of longitudinal data and confounding variables. — [CHD](https://healthdesign.org/knowledge-repository/centralized-vs-decentralized-nursing-stations-effects-nurses%E2%80%99-functional-use)
- Lee 2020 used agent-based simulation of 8 layouts. — [CHD KPS](https://healthdesign.org/sites/default/files/article-pdfs/Lee-2020-CHD-KPS.pdf)
- **Reviews:** Ulrich, Zimring, Zhu, DuBose, Seo et al. (2008), "A Review of the Research Literature on Evidence-Based Healthcare Design," *HERD* 1(3):61–125; CHD EBD white paper — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/49686913_A_Review_of_the_Research_Literature_on_Evidence-Based_Healthcare_Design); [CHD](https://www.healthdesign.org/sites/default/files/LitReviewWP_FINAL.pdf). Also Fay, Cai & Real 2019 and Jimenez et al. 2019 systematic reviews of DNS (above).
- A Herman Miller research summary, "Measuring How Far Nurses Walk: A Step in the Right Direction," gives an industry overview of methods. — [Herman Miller PDF](https://www.hermanmiller.com/content/dam/hermanmiller/documents/research_topics/Nurse_Walking.pdf)

### Inferences
- The direction of evidence is robust for two findings: decentralizing supplies and medications reduces walking, and DNS alone does not, while costing teamwork. Magnitudes are not comparable across studies because units (miles per 8, 10 or 12 h; steps; feet per task) and methods differ.
- Pedometer-based "miles per shift" figures likely overestimate work-related walking compared with RTLS.

### Gaps
- I found no randomized or multi-site controlled layout comparison after Hendrich 2008.
- I could not retrieve formal quality ratings, such as GRADE, from the CHD or AHRQ reviews.
- I found no 2026 studies in search results.
